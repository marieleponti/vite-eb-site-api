Broadly speaking, there were two different moments where the adoption of biometric identification technologies for migration control was introduced in the region as a result of US assistance. During the decade of the 2010s, Department of State Bureau of International Narcotics and Law Enforcement Affairs (DoS-INL) was involved in funding the development of Automated Fingerprint Identification Systems (AFIS) in Central America, justified as a means to fight organized crime and counternarcotics. Later on, during the 2017-2019 period, US assistance focused on the expansion of the previously mentioned identification systems through the introduction of Automated Biometric Identification Systems (ABIS), a multimodal large-scale biometric identification tool. Around this period there was also a shift in the narrative that included as a priority the identification of undocumented migrants. The programs for the adoption of these biometric identification systems has sought to:

  • Expand biometrics enrollment of migrants crossing the region
  • Develop multimodal and scalable biometrics identification systems
  • Achieve interoperability, aiming for cross-comparing data for “pre-screening” of detained migrants in countries of transit as well as at the US border

Information transparency and accountability concerning the actual use of such systems is narrow. The evidence known so far suggests that these objectives have not been executed totally in the region, and that biometric identification of detained migrants does not happen in a generalized way.

Mexico

The National Institute of Migration (INM or INAMI) has been the principal recipient of migration-related biometric identification systems in Mexico. US funding has come mainly through DoS-INL as well as the Department of Homeland Security (DHS). Funding in this aspect has focused mainly on three areas: capacity building for the collection of biometric data by provisioning hardware for infield encounters; database building for processing, storing, and searching biometric data; and data-sharing interoperability within internal systems and between Mexico and the US.

2010 early adoption of biometric identification

In 2010 DoS-INL Narcotics Affairs Section in Mexico published an acquisition plan for expanding INAMI’s Integrated System for Immigration Operations (SIOM, by its Spanish initials) to incorporate biometric technology to track all persons entering and leaving the country as well as encountered at interior checkpoints. In December of the same year Sarakki was awarded a 3.3 million contract for reengineering the SIOM that included interfacing with a centralized biometric database (Motor Biométrico Central or MBC) that would enable the use of biometric technology to control travelers entering Mexico or who are detained at a migratory station, as well as to exchange information with other Mexican authorities including the Federal Police and the intelligence (CISEN, now CNI), as well as US government agencies.

A year later in 2011, through subcontracts Alutiiq provided hardware and software for biometric identification, including a network for biometric enrollment and verification through static kiosks that were deployed in entry/exit systems at three Mexican Airports (Los Cabos, Cancun, and Mexico City) as part of the Global Entry program, as well as the integration of INAMI’s biometric systems to create the MBC.

According to a presentation from US DHS, by 2012 US support enabled INAMI biometric data collection, storing, and matching for biometric identity management at migrant stations nationwide. INAMI reported having 167 biometric enrollment devices, of which 84 were in detention centers (estaciones migratorias). By the end of that year, the MBC held 930 biometric records. The database system used for this purpose is described in the 2015 renewal as “created by CITI” and technical specifications show it is based on CITI’s ManageID “identity management” platform. Alutiiq was awarded another contract for the period 2014-2015 for US$15.1 million. This initiative known as the “INAMI project” included the dotation of servers and equipment for data centers to enable matching, storing, and sharing of biometric information. According to a former employee of Alutiiq, the intended system focused on the exchange of biometric information with the US government and the project included 120 servers, along with services for maintenance and backup support for 2 years. Apparently, this system, called SICOM donated under the Merida Initiative line of Secure Border and Ports, was held under Plataforma Mexico, a central government technological and intelligence system focused on security operations, managed by the Secretary of Security.

By November 2014 Mexico ceased to collect biometric data from detained migrants. In 2015 the biometric identification for border control initiative was relaunched. This time DoS-INL approached DHS to lead the project. Around this period US assistance for the focus of development for the biometric identification systems in Mexico shifted from transnational crime and drug trafficking towards the collection of irregular migrants’ biometric data as well as achieving interoperability with the US systems.

Towards interoperability (2017–2024)

According to a presentation by DHS made in October of 2017, INAMI lacked capacity to store and analyze their biometric data from the approximately 200,000 migrants encountered per year. During this biometric identification capacity development iteration, US assistance was pursued to achieve collection, storage, and search capacity as well as interoperability with US systems. According to DHS in a trial of this concept that happened in the year 2015, 26,433 biometric profiles were cross-referenced with US biometric databases. According to the authority, 15,295 profiles resulted in matches, of which 83.3% contained “derogatory information”.

In 2017 the INAMI, the Secretary of State (SEGOB), and DHS agreed on a Biometric Data Sharing Program (BDSP) to share and access biometric data from detained migrants in possession of both governments. As a previous step to interoperability, biometric data gathered from irregular migrants being detained in Mexico was transferred to the US government in bulk to become part of the IDENT database, becoming available to US entities having access to such systems. Accordingly, INAMI was able to run queries on the information transferred to the IDENT database.

This new agreement crystallized the shift of biometric identification infrastructure use for migration control. As shown in the objectives that the agreement follows, biometric identification in this context became directly linked to the facilitation of decisions related to:

  • Enforcing or administering immigration laws.
  • Addressing acts that would constitute a crime rendering a Third Country national inadmissible or removable.
  • Adjudication of a person’s admissibility for visas, admission to the territory or other immigration benefits as well as the determination for removal of a person.

Following the establishment of the BDSP, the DOS-INL Mexico division sought to develop and centralize the Mexican biometric system to turn it into an enhanced, scalable, and multimodal system. This project, known as Digitus, aimed to achieve interoperability between biometric systems in possession of other authorities in Mexico dealing with security and policing outside of migration management. This project aimed to “emulate” or achieve in Mexico something like the IDENT/HART system. General Dynamics Information Technology (GDIT) was awarded US$15.3 million for building this biometric-based identity management system. According to information on the request for the development of Digitus the envisioned system would have capabilities such as the following:

  1. Interfacing and interoperability with up to 6 other Mexican Biometric system applications and US Biometric Systems within the FBI, DHS (IDENT), DoD, and other systems as required.
  2. Biometric identification, verification, identity management, external biometric system search and notification services for face, finger, iris, palm voice, and latent fingerprint biometric modalities, including search by “one or more biometric images” and unique identifiers.
  3. Verification and update where identity has “been previously encountered”
  4. Search of external databases and notification services for alerting users when a monitored identity is updated.
  5. Support for 30 million transactions per year, scalable to approximately 55 million.

According to the DHS presentation on BDSP with Mexico, true interoperability with the government of Mexico was achieved in November 2017.

For the update of biometric information gathering DoS-INL awarded a contract for a potential of US$12.1 million of which only 9.9 were allocated between 2018 and 2020, for maintenance and operation of the BDSP in Mexico. Under this contract technology possibly to be used by “field agents” was provided: mobile biometric kits, fingerprint scanners along with other equipment, as well as iris cameras for kiosks. One of the subcontractors was WCC who provided an initial 12-month operational & maintenance support on the biometric matcher provided from 2011 through 2015 and in 2021 consultancy services for US$3.8 million. This suggests that WCC’s platform became a key integrator between US and US-sponsored Mexican biometric systems. According to DHS reports by 2020 biometric data sharing was “fully deployable” at 56 migration locations across Mexico.

A DoS solicitation of IT Solutions worth US$350 million designated to Alutiiq ES to be allocated between 2020 and 2025 included a contract covering equipment for “83 INM migration control checkpoints located throughout eight states in Mexico”. It is unclear whether such equipment included biometric identification technology.

It seems up to 2020 Digitus was still in a “proof of concept” phase. The system was owned by DHS and loaned to INM. In February 2023 Digitus appears to have been donated to the Secretary of Security and Citizen Protection (SSPC) in Mexico. There is no public information on how biometric data collected by INM is being treated, whether such data is being shared with the US, and or how this happens. It seems it happens at some key moments rather than all the time. For example, in 2019 in the context of migrant caravans, the government of Mexico shared biometric information from nearly 12 thousand people who had access to humanitarian visas to be cross-referenced with US databases.

According to a FOI request directed to the INAMI, the BDSP agreement signed in 2017 is still in force, further information on the number of biometric queries has been determined as confidential information for national security reasons. INAMI declared that the collection of biometric data happens at 6 migrant detention facilities located at Tapachula, Chiapas; Iztapalapa, Ciudad de México; Chetumal, Quintana Roo; San Luis Potosí, San Luis Potosí; Hermosillo, Sonora; Acayucan, Veracruz and Zacatecas, Zacatecas. According to INM between 2018 and June 2024 402,621 biometric holdings have been transferred, yet there is no certainty to which authority such transfers have occurred.

El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras

US assistance for the adoption of biometric identification infrastructures in El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras has happened in three key periods. The first comprehends the development of AFIS for law enforcement agencies under the Central America Regional Security Initiative (CARSI) in 2009. As part of its institution-building program, the FBI assisted countries in developing fingerprint and biometric capabilities under the Central American Fingerprint Exploitation Initiative (known as CAFE). This program aimed to “collect and store existing criminal biometric data and fingerprint records from Mexico, El Salvador, Guatemala, Belize, and Honduras” to incorporate them into the FBI’s Criminal Justice Information Services (CJIS) database.

The second phase of adoption of these technologies occurred in the context of the US’s biometrics data-sharing partnership and the development of multimodal biometric identification systems by national migration authorities around 2019. During this period the US coordinated and promoted collaboration and data sharing on border security and migration between law enforcement and intelligence agencies in Central America and Mexico. In 2018 Mexico, El Salvador, Honduras, and Guatemala formed the Joint Border Intelligence Group (GCIF) in collaboration with the US, allowing “CBP and US law enforcement access to participating countries’ criminal databases and histories of suspected gang members and criminals entering into or already in the United States”.

In 2019 security ministers of El Salvador, Honduras, Guatemala, and the DHS announced their intention to expand and consolidate the use of existing communications platforms, aligning technology platforms to achieve interoperability and enhancing the use of GCIF as a regional model for information exchange. In 2019 the signature of BDSPs triggered investment for the development of multimodal biometric systems for migration authorities. DoS-INL had a clear role in developing biometric-based migration management systems in El Salvador, Honduras, and Guatemala.

The third development of this surveillance infrastructure happened under the collaborative migration management policies from 2021 to 2024 in the context of the Los Angeles Declaration, which in discourse pursued promoting a “safe, orderly, and humane migration” by allegedly addressing the root causes of irregular migration, expanding the legal pathways to protection and restoring asylum in the US southern border.

Guatemala

In 2010 DoS-INL provided the Guatemalan National Civil Police (PNC) with an AFIS System under a contract with the company Cogent Systems Inc., capable of centrally storing over one million fingerprints. In 2013, DoS-INL purchased additional equipment for the expansion of the AFIS System to “22 other locations”. Up until this point, the system was in use by various Guatemalan Institutions, including the Ministry of Government under which the General Directorate of Migration existed until 2020. In 2015 a further US$141,000 was invested to acquire additional hardware for the system.

The signing of BDSPs led to an expansion of the biometric infrastructure for migration management in Guatemala. In 2019 the authority dedicated to migration management, the Dirección General de Migración (DGM) purchased licensing for a large-scale multimodal biometric system. According to the contract requirements the intended system would be able to facilitate fingerprint, facial, iris, palm, and voice with 1:1 and 1:n matching capabilities. The system provided was Megamatcher, developed by the Lithuanian company Neurotechnology, through the intermediary General Security SA. Additional purchases related to biometric systems in 2019 by the DGM pursued:

  1. Centralization of the migration information system into a Migration Control System (SIOM).
  2. Expansion of border biometric data collection through the purchase of 300-high definition cameras, as well as the purchase of 11 Kojak fingerprint scanners to be used for “passport and migration control”.

In addition to this, under the BDSP Guatemala received from the US over US$500,000 in software and equipment between May 2021 and February 2024. The donation included Kojak fingerprint scanners for border entry points, statistical analysis software, and computing and biometric equipment. In 2020 DoS-INL provided biometric workstations under a contract with Alutiiq Information Management for US$187,000 to provide equipment to facilitate the collection of biometric and biographical data from individuals encountered by the Guatemalan immigration authorities.

In 2022 DoS-INL published a statement of work for the update of Guatemala’s AFIS system. According to the statement, the update would include the provision of “interconnection between AFIS systems in the Northern Triangle countries” to get “information about the people with criminal records who are in the AFIS criminal database of each participant country.” This was provided under a contract for US$472,00 awarded to the company Grupo Vision Inversiones Guatemaltecas SA. In December of that same year, the AFIS Secura system was launched by PNC, the Ministry of Government, DoS-INL officials, and the US Embassy. The system funded by DoS-INL unified the AFIS database with neighboring countries and claimed to identify individuals through fingerprints, tattoos, scars, and height. Since 2022 Grupo Vision has supplied 113 biometric devices to different units of the PNC.

El Salvador

An AFIS system was donated by the US in 2009 to El Salvador for use in law enforcement. Initially, the system included a main central system and 10 terminals and had as objective to achieve the “automation of all ten fingerprints of people who have been detained in the country”. The biometric system was updated in 2011-2012 under a contract awarded to 3M Cogent for US$1.3 million with the assistance of the Government of Japan to increase storage capacity and points of access. Later on, in 2015 DoS-INL solicited an expansion of the existing AFIS system and in 2016 an additional contract for US$57,100 was awarded to the same company for additional biometric scanner capabilities for El Salvador’s National Civil Police (PNC).

In 2021 the PNC further expanded its biometric identification capabilities by purchasing from Grupo Vision de El Salvador SA de CV two biometric enrollment workstations with fingerprint and facial capture capabilities, connected to the AFIS system, for US$759,423. The contract specified compliance with NIST and FBI standards.

In 2016 the General Directorate of Immigration and Nationality (GDIN) listed in its assets inventory a Megamatcher AFIS system. In that same year the Ministry of Justice and Public Security (MJPS), who has administrative responsibility for the GDIN acquired from General Security SV an AFIS server worth US$170,000 and licenses for its use worth US$20,000. By November 2018 the General Directorate of Immigration and Nationality (DGME) operated two AFIS servers with fingerprint and face recognition and stored 47 million fingerprints and 1.9 million facial captures. With partial financing from the US government DGME purchased an additional AFIS server and 75 licenses for its Megamatcher system from General Security SV for a total of US$207,572.13. The system was intended to be used in migration management, aiming at the “improvement of monitoring of [the] regular flow of people entering and leaving via airports, land borders, and sea ports”. The contract requirements also speak of the need to “capture and electronically send biometric characteristics of all people who cross the borders” in adherence to the Central American Customs Union and to the data-sharing collaboration with partner countries.

Under the current administration’s anti-gang security model “Territorial Control Plan” (Plan Control Territorial) the MJPS acquired mobile and fingerprint scanners from the supplier General Security worth US$38,000. This contract also required compliance with NIST and FBI standards and included a technology called “FaceID”, suggesting having facial recognition capabilities.

In 2023 DGME awarded a contract to General Security SV worth US$65,921.32 for the maintenance of AFIS servers “Megamatcher 6 and 7”, and in 2024 purchased 43 fingerprint scanners for US$50,560.99.

Honduras

Shortly after the establishment of the National Migration Institute (INM) of Honduras in 2014 a plan to develop a Comprehensive Biometric Migration Control System to host AFIS and facial recognition was published. Later on, in 2015 the Institute issued an international public bid for acquiring a migratory control system as well as a system for the issuance of passports. There is no further information publicly available on the confirmation of the company who provided this system but it appears to be 3M Cogent Technology.

In 2014 local media reports showed that Grupo Vision supplied Honduras INM biometric scanners for four airports in the country. According to the article up to September of 2014 Honduras had registered biometrics for more than 45 thousand people. In 2017 Honduras INM bought from Grupo Vision Honduras approximately US$940,000 in equipment, licenses, technical support, communications, installation and maintenance services for its biometric migration control system.

It appears that some years later, in 2016 and in 2021 Honduras INM purchased biometric equipment for its regional offices. In 2022 the Honduran Migration Institute issued a public bid for the procurement of equipment, including fingerprint scanners for its 32 migration control stations, although there is no confirmation on the occurrence of such a procedure, the procurement appears to have been discontinued.

In 2018 Honduras Migration Institute launched a program for the “Modernization, Improvement, Management and Operation of the Migration Control System and Emission of Honduran Passports”. By 2023 the Institute had 13 different information systems including a Biometric Migration Control System, part of its Comprehensive Migration System (Sistema Integral Migratorio, ‘SIM’). The institute has two data centers (DNI and CCG) connected with its offices and delegations, its Center for Assistance to Irregular Migrants and for Assistance to Returned Migrants. In 2022 the Institute enrolled 188,269 irregular migrants in its Biometric Migration Control System, and expects to increase this number to 200,000 during 2024.

In 2023 the Migration Institute issued an international public bid to increase its biometric identification capabilities by purchasing “hardware, software and supplies to implement an immigration control system with fingerprint and facial recognition”. Technical requirements published in the bid describe a cloud-based IT system to be installed in the Institute data center, capable of supporting 750,000 daily transactions. The contract includes 20 fixed ‘control stations’ and four mobile stations. This contract was awarded to the company Representaciones e Importaciones de Honduras S.A. a local representative of Dermalog Identification Systems GMBH from Germany for approximately US$10 million.
