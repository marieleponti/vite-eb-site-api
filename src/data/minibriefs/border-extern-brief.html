In 1981 the US government negotiated an agreement with Haiti to stop “clandestine migration” to the US. In 1981, President Jimmy Carter issued Proclamation 4865 and Executive Order 12324, which authorized the U.S. Coast Guard to intercept ships carrying irregular migrants. These migrants were denied entry if U.S. authorities determined they did not qualify for refugee status. Following Haiti’s 1991 coup d’état, President George H.W. Bush expanded this policy by signing Executive Order 12,807. This order allowed the Coast Guard to intercept and return vessels heading to the U.S. without requiring any prescreening of passengers, effectively intensifying the interdiction efforts. When this practice, called Sale, was challenged the Supreme Court resolved that legislation did not protect aliens in international waters. This is one of the first examples of border externalization in the Americas.

Nowadays, border externalization has increased and diversified in the region. Through non-democratic agreements, training of security forces, donation of surveillance equipment, support and economic pressure, and the digitalization of access to asylum, the US has coerced governments in the region to contain people on the move who seek to reach the United States. This situation has contributed to the process of militarization, criminalization and securitization of border control in the region, aggregating to the vulnerability and harm faced by people on the move.

This research brief covers key historical events and presents some of the main instruments—government agreements and migration policies—that have played an important role in border externalization on the continent.

Mexico

In many aspects, how the border has been externalized towards Mexico has served as the model replicated for other countries in the region. This is evident, for example, in the assistance for the establishment of security forces focused on migration control, as well as in the adoption of Biometric Data Sharing Programs (BDSPs). On the other hand, certain differences stand out in the involvement of Mexico as a promoter of border externalization in the region, acting as a link and intermediary of the US. These dynamics will be explored in the following sections.

Development of security forces for migration management (1993–2000)

One of the key events in Mexico’s migration policy shift towards securitization and containment was the creation of the National Migration Institute (INM or INAMI, by its Spanish initials) in 1993. The Institute was created through a presidential decree in the context of the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) signed a year before. The decree included the establishment of “migration stations” (migrant detention centers), as well as the foundation of the Federal Migration Police. From its inception, the INAMI has mainly sought to deport and detain undocumented migrants.

In 1996, Mexico hosted the first edition of Proceso Puebla, a regional conference on the theme of migration. The conference called for “shared responsibility” for the migrant flow crossing the region towards the US border. The conference action plans emphasized security topics such as human trafficking and migrant smuggling, leaving out issues such as migrant rights and development that were originally included in the conference declaration. Throughout the years, this conference has revolved around issues such as the adoption of a framework for orderly and regular migration, repatriation and reinsertion of migrants, regional containment of undocumented migrants, as well as the adoption of interoperable digital infrastructure for data collection and sharing between Mexico, Guatemala, El Salvador, and Honduras. The conference happened behind closed doors without the inclusion of activists, academics or representatives of directly affected populations and did not address issues such as the disappearance of migrants in transit in the region.

In 1998 under Operación Sellamiento the governments of Mexico and the U.S enforced coordinated actions on Mexico’s Southern Border to stop migration. Mirroring Operation Gatekeeper, this strategy followed the same objective of “prevention through deterrence”. The 1990s marked a shift in Mexico’s focus on migration policy, refocusing on control of its southern border.

Securitization (2001–2013)

As part of the “securitization turn” after the 9/11 attacks, in 2001 Mexico implemented Plan Sur, a migration management policy aimed at increasing surveillance of the southern border through the creation of inspection and control checkpoints, the strengthening inter-institutional cooperation with security forces against undocumented traffic, and an increase in the number of undocumented migrants detained. It has been suggested that Mexico negotiated better treatment for Mexican immigrants in the US in return for controlling the transit of migrants from Central America crossing Mexico. The United States invested 11 million in supporting Plan Sur, accounting for half of the program’s budget.

In March 2005, the Security and Prosperity Partnership of North America (SPP), a supra-national level dialogue between the United States, Mexico, and Canada, established national security as a central issue in their relationship. This partnership adopted a security vision advanced after the 2001 attacks that conceptualizes US security as a North American regional issue. A RAND article published in 2007 on the topic of ungoverned territories as a risk factor for terrorism presents the southern border of Mexico as a highly dangerous area and space for human and drug trafficking to flourish, as well as a potential territory of control for the Mara gangs that were ravaging Central America. This reflects the narrative developed during this period, in which controlling the Mexican border and irregular migration from the Global South became national security issues. In May 2005, just two months after SPP, the Mexican government redefined INAMI as a national security organism. This change significantly reduced transparency in how the agency operates, as much of its information was now classified and kept confidential.

The Merida Initiative, a 2009 security aid program between the US and Mexico signed as the Mexican government was escalating its war against the cartels, included as one of its main pillars the creation of a 21st Century Border. US support for migration control under the Merida Initiative resulted in the implementation of a southern border security plan, Plan Frontera Sur (2014) which by 2015 included the establishment of 12 naval bases in Mexico’s rivers as well as three security sections that stretch more than 100 miles north of Mexico’s southern border. By 2018, the US had donated around US$100 million in equipment and training for securing Mexico’s southern border through the Merida Initiative. While Andrés Manuel López Obrador’s administration (2018–2024) criticized the war against the cartels and the Mérida Initiative, reducing federal security cooperation with the United States, migration enforcement was the exception. Both López Obrador and his successor Claudia Sheinbaum have continued to collaborate with the United States on initiatives for the containment, deportation and detention of undocumented migrants.

Rise of the Vertical Border (2014–2017)

In 2014, shortly after former Vice President Biden held a meeting with the presidents of Mexico, Honduras, Guatemala, and El Salvador on migrant containment in the region following an acute increase in the number of unaccompanied minors detained in the US southern border, Plan Frontera Sur was announced in Mexico. A day after the announcement of this contention policy plan, President Obama requested US Congress support for the Mexican policy for US$86 million. Most of the funding went to INAMI, including US$32 million for surveillance equipment and training, comprising biometric kiosks and non-intrusive inspection tools used in Mexico’s southern border.

Plan Frontera Sur resulted in a spike of 90 percent in migrant detentions in Mexico together with an increase in the number of crimes committed against people on the move. As containment became more harsh, migration routes had to shift towards more remote and dangerous areas. One of the central pillars of Frontera Sur, centered on improving the security infrastructure in Mexico’s southern border, included a plan to create an interoperable system to share fingerprints, photos, and other sensitive information from migrants with the government of Guatemala.

In 2017, the INAMI, the Mexican Secretary of the Interior, and the US Secretary of Homeland Security signed a Statement of Cooperation concerning biometric immigration information sharing. This agreement outlined a framework for the sharing of biometric data of third-country undocumented migrants through “biometric queries”. This agreement served as a blueprint for the successor BDSPs signed with the governments of Honduras, El Salvador, Guatemala, and the US. After this agreement was signed, the narrative behind the use of biometric identification in the migration context in the region shifted from security to irregular migration management.

Further militarization and Safe Third Country status (2018–2022)

In February 2016, Haitian migrants who arrived at the San Ysidro point of entry were required to “wait in line” before presenting themselves at the US border to ask for asylum. At this time Mexican authorities were in charge of this asylum restraint strategy, in which lists with the information of people seeking to present themselves to ask for asylum in the US were used to order the applicants. In 2018 this strategy evolved and became what was later known as metering, in which asylum seekers arriving on the US southern border were regularly turned away by CBP officials before arriving at the border to wait in Mexico for their turn. Waiting times spanned from some days to several months depending on the concentration of people at the points of entry.

Afterwards, different actors from place to place, including the participation of government authorities, NGOs, and migrant shelters held and managed lists where order in which people on the move could present themselves at US border to ask for asylum. Metering resulted in longer waiting times in Mexico for people looking for asylum in the US, exposing them to security risks and leaving them in a state of exception with limited access to rights including health, education, housing, and others. This strategy also impacted humanitarian organizations’ capacities, since they faced more complex needs from the people they assisted and on top of that, even had to manage metering lists in some cases, which put them at risk.

“[Metering] It also placed us, as leaders of these organizations, in a highly exposed and vulnerable position. If you have the ability to help people cross, you become a target for those who might demand your assistance. For example, at one shelter, masked and armed men showed up, making such demands. […] On one occasion, we had to hide 18 Haitians because of these very issues—access to lists of people planning to cross made them targets, and they were ultimately threatened at gunpoint.” Judith Cabrera de la Rocha – Director at Border Line Crisis Center. Tijuana, February 2024.

President Andres Manuel López Obrador had promised several times since his election campaign to receive migrants with “open arms”. In mid-January 2019 the “Hunger Caravan” that departed from Honduras was received at the Mexican southern border by government officials who offered visitor cards for humanitarian reasons (TVRH by its initials in Spanish) documents to cross Mexico as well as a permit to stay for a year in Mexico and have access to health services, work, education and residence.

This policy remained in effect until February. By that time, the Mexican government had issued 13,270 TVRH (Temporary Visitor Cards for Humanitarian Reasons) cards. The program was restricted to the border area along the Suchiate River between Mexico and Guatemala and was not implemented nationwide. This led to a concentration of migration flows in the region. Civil society groups criticized the program for lacking a clear migration policy, offering limited access, and having lengthy processing times. It has been argued that it functioned more as a “temporary limit” or containment strategy rather than a genuine solution.

In practice, this approach towards migration did not last long. Later in 2019, former US president Trump threatened to impose tariffs on trade coming from Mexico until undocumented migration flow was stopped from arriving at the US southern border. A week later, Mexico agreed to take “unprecedented steps to increase enforcement to curb irregular migration”. This resulted in the deployment of 6,500 National Guard agents to the southern border and 15,000 to the northern border, as well as accepting asylum seekers being turned away daily from the U.S. borders to await asylum adjudication under Migration Protection Protocols.

In 2020 in the context of the eruption of the COVID-19 pandemic, the US government issued an order suspending asylum related measures in the border, claiming foreigners entering US territory posed a health threat. As borders remained closed, migrants arriving at the US southern border were expelled to Mexico to wait under metering limbo. These restrictions were made possible under Title 42, an emergency provision that allows for the limitation of migration to protect public health.

In 2021 the governments of the US and Mexico agreed to the Bicentennial Framework for Security, Public Health, and Safe Communities. This agreement replaced the Merida Initiative. The actual text of the agreement has not been made public, but Mexican army emails leaked by the hacktivist organization Guacamaya include related documents. According to these documents, one of the three main goals of the agreement is preventing transborder crimes. This goal seeks coordination between the signatories in order to:

  1. Achieve secure transport and commerce
  2. Reduce firearms traffic
  3. Dismantle transnational criminal organizations
  4. Reduce human and migrant trafficking

The Bicentennial agreement includes migration control strategies as components of almost all of its objectives. It seems the agreement seeks to further develop the use of biometric identification as a migration control strategy. For example, in order to reduce transnational human trafficking, it seeks to “strengthen the legal regime related to human trafficking and expand the exchange of biometric information in the region” and to “develop memorandums of understanding and related policy and privacy documents to enable the exchange of biometric information with Central America.” The agreement also seems to expand the securitization of migration management, as “illegal migrant detention” is one of the tasks it assigns to Mexican security forces. The text also seeks to increase other types of data sharing related to migration control as it includes the “development and strengthening of interoperability between the data systems of the United States and Mexico” regarding direct flights landing in Mexico.

Introduction and Cessation of CBP One (2023–2024)

In January 2023 mandatory use of CBP One for accessing asylum requests in the southern border. The mobile application developed by the US Department of Homeland Security originally designed for customs and precheck of visitor – land travelers, for example, could use the app to submit information ahead of their crossing, and air travelers could request and schedule an inspection of biological and agricultural products upon their arrival into the country. Based on alleged efficiency and security reasons, this digitalization of access to asylum was maintained and expanded during the Biden administration. In practice, this digital infrastructure has served as a containment and deterrence tool as migrants face long waits in Mexico –in a state of insecurity and exception– before being able to present themselves to a point of entry to ask for asylum in the US. During the 1.5 years in which CBP One was in use, the Mexican government largely denied responsibility for the humanitarian needs of migrants awaiting appointments, focusing narrowly on providing access to the Internet and, less frequently, to migrant shelters.

In May of 2023, 40 migrants died at a migrant detention center in Ciudad Juárez, Chihuahua, as they were kept under lock amid a fire that was ignited as part of a migrant protest. This protest was not an isolated case; until march 2024 during AMLO’s administration 41 riots and four other fires were ignited as a protest against the inhumane conditions faced at INM detention centers, and there are many additional examples from years prior. The government responded to the tragedy by blaming low-ranking officials and the victims themselves. Before the case went public, the Executive Commission for Victim Assistance urged a survivor to accept an alternative resolution to prevent INM director Francisco Garduño Yáñez from being implicated in the legal proceedings. As of the time of this publication, no government official has been sentenced. The director of INM has retained his position, was acquitted of charges, and no significant measures have been implemented to address or repair the damages caused.

In October 2023 high-level representatives from Belize, Colombia, Costa Rica, Cuba, El Salvador, Haiti, Honduras, Mexico, Panama, and Venezuela held a migration summit in Palenque, Chiapas, agreeing to implement 13 joint actions to allegedly address push factors and human rights together with trade, international sanctions and the coordination of governments to ask for more flexibility regarding working visas to work in the United States.

In June 2024, United States President Biden issued an executive order allowing the immediate expulsion of migrants encountered at the US southern border, including from third countries, to Mexico when the threshold of 2,500 daily detained people at any point of entry is crossed. This practice further limits the access to asylum of people crossing the border to ask for protection. Just a day after this announcement hundreds of migrants were violently evicted from a campsite at a public square in Mexico City. The operation happened with the coordination of INAMI and local police, under the new leadership of President Claudia Sheinbaum, who took office that month after serving as Mexico City’s mayor from 2018–2023. Local activists and organizations denounced the eviction for violating human rights as well as Mexico City’s constitutional protections for migrants. Historically the city has been considered a “sanctuary city” for migrants, but things started to shift under Sheinbaum’s mayoral administration and may get worse during her presidency.

The situation for migrants has grown increasingly complex due to the militarization of migration policies and the criminalization of the right to migrate. This is part of a broader securitization approach, compounded by collusion between criminal groups and authorities. We discussed this with Magdalena Silva, director of CAFEMIN, one of the largest migrant shelters in Mexico City:

Yo [he visto que está] más, más fuerte de dos años para acá. Más fuerte. Ya el tema del crimen organizado, el tema de todo el trabajo conludido, ¿no? Del Instituto Nacional de Migración, de la Guardia Nacional, de etcétera, etcétera, con el crimen organizado. Y la gente te dice, nos secuestraron, me violaron, nos quitaron todo lo que traíamos, los de migración nos bajaron y nos quitaron hasta los celulares. Entonces, está grave. La gente te lo dice. Los niños te lo dicen. Entonces, tampoco nosotros, bueno, yo al menos en los 13 años ya de cafemin no había visto esto. Aquí, llegando, se nos ha muerto gente. Que de veras parecía que nomás esperaba llegar y se nos ha muerto gente. De cómo viene una embolia, un paro, un estómago cardíaco, un derrame cerebral. ¿Ve? De cómo viene. Embarazos y alto riesgo que los bebés se nos mueren también. Y es todo lo que traen, todo lo que viven, todo lo que les sucede. Y yo lo he visto de dos años para acá.

“I [have seen that it has become] much worse over the past two years. Much worse. The issue of organized crime, the issue of all the collusion, right? Between the National Migration Institute, the National Guard, and so on, with organized crime. And people tell us, they kidnapped us, they raped me, they took everything we had, migration officers stopped us and even took our phones. So, it’s serious. People tell you this. Children tell you this. So, even we—well, at least in my 13 years with CAFEMIN, I had never seen this before. Here, upon arrival, we’ve had people die. It really seemed like they were just waiting to arrive, and then they died. From things like a stroke, cardiac arrest, a heart attack, a cerebral hemorrhage. You see? From what they’ve been through. Pregnancies and high-risk situations where the babies have also died. And it’s everything they carry with them, everything they’ve lived through, everything that happens to them. And I’ve seen this over the past two years.” Cafemin, July 2024. Mexico City.

El Salvador, Guatemala and Honduras

Security assistance and funding have played an important role in border externalization in El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras. The US has provided training and equipment, in part to encourage these countries to pursue US security interests. This dynamic has included the conditioning of further funding to migrant containment, as shown in an excerpt from a hearing before the Committee of Foreign Relations of the US Senate in September, 2019:

The President and the Secretary [of State] have asked the governments of El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras to take concrete steps to stem irregular migration to the United States. The President and the Secretary have said that the crucial metric is a decrease in the number of migrants apprehended at the US-Mexico border. As the President has made clear, there cannot be a resumption of assistance until our partners demonstrably do their part to reduce out-migration.

Funding and Assistance for Security Forces Fighting Organized Crime (2006–2007)

In 2007 the FBI and El Salvador’s National Civic Police or PNC (by its initials in Spanish) financed and implemented an initiative to deal with the spread of Central American gangs’ presence in the US. This partnership known as the Transnational Anti-Gang or Centro Antipandillas Transnational (CAT) worked towards the identification and tracking of alleged gang members in El Salvador as well as with partners throughout Central America, through the gathering and transferring of personal and biographical data to the FBI headquarters. The identification of gang members was aided by the Central American Fingerprint Exploitation Initiative or CAFÉ, an initiative promoted by the FBI in 2006 in the region to gather and share biometric information from criminal records in possession of the governments of Mexico, El Salvador, and Guatemala, Belize, and Honduras. Data and fingerprints gathered under such initiatives were incorporated into the FBI’s CJIS database.

The focus and work of CAT evolved from managing the identification of alleged gang members in El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras, to also surveilling deportees from the US labeled as criminals under the Criminal History Records Information Sharing program (CHIS). This is part of the securitization turn observed regionally, where institutions originally dedicated to security issues have become involved in migration management. Under CHIS, migrants labeled as felons are held under surveillance in their country of origin after being deported. Information shared under this program has resulted in extrajudicial killings by local police of deportees labeled as criminals.

Early development of migration control security forces (2013–2015)

The creation of armed forces dedicated specifically to managing migration marked a new phase in border externalization. In 2013, US Army South, Army National Guard Soldiers from Texas, US Border Service, and Western Hemisphere Institute for Security Cooperation personnel trained the newly created Guatemalan Interagency Task Force Tecun Uman (IATF, by its Spanish initials), dedicated to controlling points of entry along Guatemala’s border with Mexico, as well as other law enforcement activities. The force has been noted as one of the top priorities in Central America for the Southern Command and US Army South. The US government contributed US$23 million to the development of the IATF.

A year later, in 2014 the US helped set up the Chorti Special Task Force, specifically dedicated to border policing in Guatemala. Support for the Special Task Force included the physical presence of a US Army Major working along with the Guatemalan military police. In 2016, the US Army donated 50 vehicles, protection equipment, and funding for the construction of an operation center for the Task Force.

Following this trend, in 2015 Honduras and Guatemala established the Especial Task Force Maya-Chorti, the first modern binational force between the two countries, dedicated to fighting organized crime, trafficking, and common crime along the two countries’ border. The force included 200 police and 190 army members from each country. The US government promised to donate US$13.4 million to the task force.

In 2016, the US Congress approved around US$130 million in assistance for nearly 20 military and police forces in El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras dedicated to border enforcement and combatting drug trafficking. In that same year, the governments of these countries established a Tri-National Anti-Gang Task Force to monitor the shared border. The joint declaration announcing the creation of the Task Force mentions the implementation of an information-sharing system between the countries together with a call for greater coordination with the CAT (Transnational Anti-Gang Center, by its Spanish initials).

2019 DHS agreements with Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador

In response to an increase in the number of migrant detentions at the US southern border in 2019, the United States sought to create a “regional compact” in Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador for migration control. This strategy pursued four pillars: (1) Combating Human Trafficking and Migrant Smuggling, (2) Countering Organized Crime and Gangs, (3) Expanding Information and Intelligence Sharing and (4) Strengthening Border Security. This strategy was externalized in part by the ratification of four agreements with Honduras, Guatemala, and El Salvador. In March 2019, prior to the signing of the agreements, the US State Department announced the cutting off of over US$400 million in foreign assistance to these Central American countries alleging they were not doing enough to prevent their citizens from migrating.

Asylum Cooperation Agreement (ACA)

During the latter half of 2019, the US government signed agreements to restrict access to asylum for citizens of Honduras, El Salvador, and Guatemala. Similar to the U.S.-Canada Safe Third Country Agreement, the Asylum Cooperative Agreements (ACAs) allowed the U.S. to send Honduran, Salvadoran, and Guatemalan asylum seekers apprehended at the U.S.-Mexico border to one of the other two Central American countries (rather than their country of origin) to seek asylum there. The adoption of these treaties externalizing asylum was declared by Acting Secretary of Homeland Security Chad F. Wolf as a “critical step in the establishment of a truly regional approach to migration”.

Civil society organizations have criticized the adoption of Safe Third Country and other similar agreements because they limit access to asylum and place people facing persecution at greater risk if they are deported, especially since El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras do not qualify as safe countries and lack adequate asylum capacity that can ensure international protection. This is particularly worrisome due to the presence of gangs such as MS-13, high homicide rates, and government oppression of political adversaries throughout the region, specially endangering vulnerable populations, including women, children, and LGBTQ+ individuals, who might face persecution or harm in these countries.

The U.S.-Guatemala Asylum Cooperative Agreement (ACA) was briefly implemented, with the U.S. sending some asylum seekers to Guatemala. However, the program encountered major logistical and legal obstacles. Meanwhile, the agreements with Honduras and El Salvador were never fully put into practice.

In February 2021, the Biden administration terminated the ACAs with El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras. According to a press release from the White House, migration control would now pivot to “safe and orderly processing” of people arriving at the US border while also addressing root causes of forced displacement and irregular migration by combating corruption and impunity in sending countries.

Border Security Arrangements (BSAs)

The BSAs focused on the deployment of CBP and ICE officials to Honduras, El Salvador, and Guatemala to advise and inform national police, border security, immigration, and customs forces. In October 2020, under Biden, DHS agents rolled out an operation to contain a caravan crossing Guatemala, directly renting passenger vans to deport Honduran migrants – including women and children. Civil society organizations denounced DHS operations for violating migrants’ rights, for putting their lives at risk by returning them to the persecution they may have fled, as well as for overreaching into foreign policy, which is the prerogative of the State Department.

Biometric Data Sharing Program (BDSP)

In fall 2019 DHS signed agreements with Honduras, El Salvador, and Guatemala to exchange biometric information of detained people on the move. BDSP agreements focus on building capacity for local biometric systems and enhancing interoperability to support bilateral information exchange. They aim to expand the collection of biometric data from individuals crossing borders in Central American countries without valid documents, while also aligning technology platforms to meet operational and information-sharing requirements.

Temporary Agricultural and Non-agricultural Workers Program Agreement

Also known as the H-2A program, this agreement renegotiated agreements for specific US employers to ask for foreigners to work in temporary positions in the agricultural sector. This type of agreement was signed with Honduras and Guatemala.

Los Angeles Declaration on Migration and Protection (2022)

In June 2022 representatives of twenty governments from the Americas and the Caribbean met to set principles for managing the movement of people across the hemisphere following a “safe, orderly, humane, and regular migration” as well as to strengthen international protection and cooperation. The Declaration focused on four principles for collaboration on migration management: (1) Supporting countries hosting refugees and displaced populations, (2) Expanding legal pathways for mobility, (3) Coordinating migration policies among countries, and (4) Providing coordinated responses to emergencies.

The Declaration uses language that reiterates the need for migration management to respect the human rights of people in transit; but in practice, this has been largely missing from the way governments have dealt with migration flows. Efforts to deal with push factors of migration have not been effective. At the same time, the profiles and places of origin of people in transit have diversified, with more than half of people arriving at the US border coming from countries beyond Central America and detentions at the US border doubling between the fiscal years of 2022 and 2023.

Coordination among countries to deal with migration in the region has focused on deepening militarization and criminalization. There has not been a substantive expansion of legal pathways for mobility, nor of access to international protection. Rather, the US and its allies have implemented further restrictions to mobility through the strengthening of their visa systems since the declaration. For example, Mexico and Costa Rica implemented new visa restrictions to people from Venezuela that resulted in more people crossing the Darien Gap into getting into North American continent.

Containment measures disguised under pathways for migration, such as the use of the CBP One application as a digital metering tool and the Safe Mobility Initiatives in countries throughout Latin America have also been adopted, but in practice they mostly serve to increase the difficulties faced by people in transit seeking protection in the US. The declaration fails to address the current motives making people leave their countries, the international protection needs demonstrated by a large proportion of people in transit as well as the human rights violations being perpetuated in the name of a “safe, orderly, humane, and regular migration.”
