Executive Summary

Corporate involvement and US funding has driven the development and deployment of biometric-based identity infrastructure used to control migration in Guatemala, Honduras, Mexico, and El Salvador. The push for biometrics collection and information sharing with Washington has increased over the past 15 years, becoming a crucial element of border externalization, by which a state shifts migration control functions, typically carried out within its own territory, to other countries, either partially or entirely. This transfer often occurs through negotiation, coercion, or political pressure exercised by destination countries onto transit and origin countries, relocating enforcement beyond national borders.

Building on preliminary findings, the Everywhere Border project commissioned financial research group FIND to conduct in-depth research on key corporate entities including Alutiiq LLC, Leidos Holdings Inc, General Dynamics Corp, Thales SA (as current owner of Cogent-branded biometric technology 1) and NEC Corp, which supply and maintain biometric systems across the target countries.

In order to prepare their report, FIND accessed information from US procurement databases, looked up the laws, rules, policies, and documents produced by government entities in the US, Mexico, Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador in relation to biometrics, collected media articles, reviewed non-governmental and intergovernmental reports, scanned news outlets, public websites, and social media, and examined corporate records.

FIND was specifically commissioned to research corporate involvement in U.S.-funded biometric capabilities in Guatemala, Honduras, Mexico, and El Salvador. These countries were selected because of their geographical and strategic importance to U.S. border externalization efforts, as well as the Biometric Data Sharing Program (BDSP) agreements they signed with the United States between 2017 and 2019.

The FIND research covers the period from 2010 to 2024. Supporting documents for this research, including contracts, corporation brochures, government agreements, and more, can be found here.

This research confirms the central role of the United States in promoting and facilitating the adoption of biometric technologies for migration management across Mexico and Central America.

Over the last 15 years, under both Republican and Democratic administrations, the US government—and in particular the Department of State and the Department of Homeland Security—has made a sustained effort to expand regional capacities for the surveillance and identification of migrants through the deployment of multimodal and scalable biometric systems.

Continued US investment in this sector makes clear biometric and personal data gathering is a central piece in a broader strategy of border externalization and enforcement in the region. It also raises critical concerns about the protection of human rights, criminalization, and the use of biometric technologies in limiting access to asylum.

The repurposing of biometric identification systems—initially introduced under the pretext of combating crime and drug trafficking—to monitor and criminalize people on the move exemplifies a clear case of function creep, defined as the expansion of a technology or practice beyond its original purposes.

Function creep has led to the expansion of surveillance to include migrant populations. It also raises broader issues around the normalization of biometric monitoring throughout the region. In this context, the repurposing of biometric technologies coincides with the creation of armed forces dedicated specifically to managing migration in Central America and the launch of Plan Frontera Sur in Mexico in 2014, which resulted in a 90 percent increase in migrant detentions in its first year.

As part of the broader push toward interoperability, biometric data collected from irregular migrants detained in Mexico has been transferred in bulk to the U.S. government. This data has been integrated into DHS’s IDENT 2 system, making it accessible to various U.S. agencies, including but not limited to those involved in migration enforcement and border security.

Although one of the ultimate objectives of the deployment of biometric identification systems used in migration control in the region has been to continuously gather and share biometric data, available evidence suggests that in practice this has happened at key strategic moments rather than continuously, at least until 2024. For instance, in 2019, during the arrival of large migrant caravans, the Mexican government transferred the biometric data of approximately 12,000 individuals—who had been issued humanitarian visas—to the U.S. for cross-referencing against American government databases.

In all countries studied, there is a lack of transparency and accountability regarding the deployment of biometric systems and the handling of sensitive data they collect and store. Available evidence indicates that full interoperability of these systems has not yet been achieved across the region, and that the collection of biometric identification from detained migrants remains uneven and inconsistent.

The widespread procurement of Neurotechnology’s MegaMatcher system reflects ongoing efforts to build interoperable infrastructures among Central American states, raising critical concerns about cross-border data sharing, oversight, and the protection of migrants’ rights.

Introduction to biometrics-based migration management infrastructures in Mexico, El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras

Broadly speaking, there have been two different moments in which U.S. assistance spurred the adoption of biometric identification technologies in the region.

The first one started around 2006, when the Department of State Bureau of International Narcotics and Law Enforcement Affairs (DoS-INL) was involved in funding the development of Automated Fingerprint Identification Systems (AFIS) in Central America. This was justified as part of the fight against organized crime and narcotics trafficking.

The second was from 2017 to 2019, when U.S. assistance was focused on expanding the fingerprint-based identification systems (AFIS), through the introduction of Automated Biometric Identification Systems (ABIS), a multimodal large-scale biometric identification tool, capable of conducting one-to-many identification procedures using multiple biometric modalities (fingerprint, iris, facial recognition). This system allows for broader surveillance applications and interoperability between diverse databases and systems, handling large volumes of data. During this period, U.S. government documents concerning the development and support of biometric identification systems began to reflect a shift in language, less in terms of security and policing, and more in the context of migration management and the facilitation of immigration decision-making.

In Mexico and Central America, the programs for the adoption of these biometric identification systems have focused on expanding biometric enrollment of migrants crossing the region, developing multimodal and scalable biometric identification systems and achieving interoperability. The overall goal is to create the possibility for continuously cross-comparing data in order to “pre-screen” detained migrants in countries of transit as well as at the U.S. border.

Mexico, proving ground for biometrics collection

The National Institute of Migration (INM or INAMI) has been the principal recipient of migration-related biometric identification systems in Mexico 3. U.S. funding has come mainly through DoS-INL as well as the Department of Homeland Security (DHS). Funding in this aspect has focused primarily on three areas: capacity building for the collection of biometric data by providing hardware for encounters in migration routes; database building for processing, storing, and searching biometric data; and data-sharing between internal systems and between Mexico and the U.S.

Biometric-based systems for migration control

In 2010, the DoS-INL Narcotics Affairs Section in Mexico published an acquisition plan for expanding the INM’s Integrated System for Immigration Operations (SIOM, by its Spanish initials) to incorporate biometric technology to track all persons entering and leaving the country as well as people encountered at interior checkpoints. In December of the same year, US based engineering firm Sarakki with a focus on border management systems, was awarded a $3.3 million contract for reengineering the SIOM that included interfacing with a centralized biometric database (Motor Biométrico Central or MBC) that would enable the use of biometric technology to collect information on travelers entering Mexico or who are detained at migratory detention facilities (euphemistically called in Mexico “estaciones migratorias” or migration stations in English), as well as to exchange information with other Mexican authorities including the Federal Police and the national intelligence agency (CISEN, now CNI), as well as US government agencies.

A year later, in 2011, the Alaskan contracting company Alutiiq, who has received around USD 1.8 billion in U.S. federal contracts since 2014, the majority from the Department of Defense, received subcontracts to provide hardware and software for biometric identification. These contracts included the creation of a network for biometric enrollment and verification through fixed kiosks that were deployed as part of entry/exit systems at three Mexican Airports (Los Cabos, Cancun, and Mexico City) as part of the Global Entry program. They also saw Alutiiq working on the integration of INM’s biometric systems to create a centralized biometric database (MBC).

According to a presentation by the DHS, by 2012 U.S. support enabled the INM to collect, store, and match biometric data at migrant detention facilities nationwide 4. At that time, the INM reported having 167 biometric enrollment devices, of which 84 were in migration detention facilities, adopted as part of its modernization plan for the period between 2006 and 2012. Such a plan, partially funded by the Mérida Initiative, sought to enhance the MBC as a tool for monitoring irregular migrants and the facilitation of frequent, authorized border crossings. By the end of 2012, the MBC held 930 biometric records.

The database system used for this purpose is described in the 2015, $1.98 million contract renewal 5 as “created by CITI” and technical specifications show it is based on CITI’s ManageID “identity management” platform. US-based Creative Information Technology Inc (‘CITI’) specializes in systems engineering and integration as well as identity management/biometrics. According to a FOIA response from the INM by February of 2022 the MBC held 480,610 identity records that include biometric data that has been gathered as part of the emission of identification cards for visiting, resident, regional visiting and border worker visitor permits 6.

Neurotechnology’s MegaMatcher system—used for fingerprint extraction, template creation, identification, and verification—is widely deployed in Mexico and across the region. Several initiatives involving the collection of biometric data and its linkage to individuals’ Unique Population Registry Code (CURP, by its Spanish acronym) also rely on MegaMatcher. For example, local civil registry offices in states such as Tabasco, Nuevo León, and Baja California Sur have awarded contracts to Biometría Aplicada S.A. de C.V. to pilot the use of biometrics for identification purposes. In addition, major federal institutions, including the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (SRE) and the Ministry of the Interior, have adopted MegaMatcher. The latter uses the system as part of Plataforma México, a relatively obscure national information exchange and intelligence platform. The widespread adoption of MegaMatcher across various government agencies reflects a broader push toward interoperability between biometric systems used for diverse government functions.

Alutiiq was awarded a major contract in 2014-2015 for $15.1 million. This initiative, known as the “INAMI project” included the donation of servers to the INM and equipment for data centers to enable matching, storing, and sharing of biometric information. According to a former employee of Alutiiq, the intended system focused on the exchange of biometric information with the U.S. government. The project included 120 servers, along with services for maintenance and backup support for two years. It appears this system, which was dubbed SICOM (Spanish acronym for “Sistema Integral de Control Migratorio”, or Migration Control Integral System), was donated to Mexico under the Merida Initiative, a multibillion dollar counternarcotics program that ran from 2007-2021, under the Secure Border and Ports budget line. It was operated under Plataforma Mexico. The system’s purpose is to allow for coordination through the exchange of information among municipal, local and federal authorities for the purpose of crime investigations. Plataforma Mexico is managed by the Secretary of Security.

By November 2014, Mexico had stopped collecting biometric data from detained migrants, and in 2015 the initiative to collect biometric identification for border control was relaunched. This time, DoS-INL approached DHS to lead the project.

Towards interoperability (2017–2024)

According to a presentation by the DHS in October of 2017, at that time the INM lacked capacity to store and analyze their biometric data from the approximately 200,000 migrants encountered every year. That led the DHS to focus US assistance on collection, storage, and search capacity as well as interoperability with U.S. systems.

According to DHS, during a trial preceding the Biometric Data Sharing program with Mexico, in 2015, 26,433 biometric profiles were cross-referenced with US biometric databases. According to the DHS, 15,295 of the cross-referenced profiles resulted in matches, of which 83.3 percent contained “derogatory information” comprised by federal alerts (1,171 profiles), arrest warrants and wanted notices (445 profiles), and flags of suspected drug smugglers (45 profiles), and gang members (32 profiles) 7. Likely, the remainder of the “derogatory information” flagged by the system refers to immigration violations.

In 2017 the INM, Mexico’s Secretary of State (SEGOB), and the DHS signed an agreement toward a Biometric Data Sharing Program (BDSP) to share and access biometric data from detained migrants. As a previous step to interoperability, biometric data gathered from irregular migrants being detained in Mexico was transferred to the U.S. government in bulk, becoming part of the IDENT database, which made it available to U.S. entities having access to such systems 8. In addition to handing biometric information to the US government, Mexico’s INM was able to run queries on the information transferred to the IDENT database.

The BDSP as well as IDENT’s Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA) appendix on international data transfers published in 2017 crystallized the shift of biometric identification infrastructure use to migration control. Both the PIA’s and the agreement’s objectives for the transfer of biometric information state the facilitation of decisions related to enforcing or administering immigration laws, addressing acts that would constitute a crime rendering a third country national inadmissible or removable, and the adjudication of a person’s admissibility for visas, admission to the territory, or other immigration benefits as well as the determination by which a person could be removed from the territory. This departs from previously stated objectives related to security and policing-focus that had characterized DoS-INL descriptions of biometric system development in the region up until this time.

Following the establishment of the BDSP in 2017, the DOS-INL Mexico division sought to develop and centralize Mexico’s biometrics system, seeking to turn it into an enhanced, scalable, and multimodal system. This project is known as Digitus, and aims to achieve interoperability between biometric systems operated by security and policing agencies in Mexico that have little to nothing to do with migration.

Digitus aimed to “emulate” or achieve in Mexico something like the IDENT system. In 2018 company General Dynamics Information Technology (GDIT), subsidiary of General Dynamics Corp, one of U.S. top five defense contractors, was awarded $15.3 million by DoS to build this biometric-based identity management system. According to the “sources sought (RFI) 9” document for the development of Digitus, the envisioned system would have five key functions:

  1. Interfacing and interoperability with up to six other Mexican biometric system applications and U.S. Biometric Systems within the FBI, DHS (IDENT), DoD, and other systems as required.
  2. Biometric identification, verification, identity management, external biometric system search, and notification services for face, finger, iris, palm voice, and latent fingerprint biometric modalities, including search by “one or more biometric images” and unique identifiers.
  3. Verification and update where an identity has “been previously encountered”
  4. Search of external databases and notification services to alert users when a monitored identity is updated.
  5. Support for 30 million transactions per year, scalable to approximately 55 million.

According to the DHS presentation on the Biometric Data Sharing Program with Mexico, true interoperability with the government of Mexico was achieved in November 2017.

Between 2018 and 2020, the DoS-INL awarded a contract for up to $12.1 million (of which only $9.9 million were allocated) for the maintenance and operation of the BDSP in Mexico. Under this contract, technology that could be used by migration enforcement agents was provided, including mobile biometric kits, fingerprint scanners, iris cameras for kiosks along with other equipment. One of the subcontractors was WCC who provided an initial 12-month operational & maintenance support and system update in 2018 on the “WCC Group Corporate’s Elise ID search and match software”, provided in 2015. In 2021 the company received an additional $3.8 million for consultancy services. This suggests that WCC’s platform became key to the integration of U.S. biometrics systems and the biometric systems sponsored by the US government in Mexico. According to DHS, by 2020 biometric data sharing was “fully deployable” at 56 “migration locations” 10 across Mexico.

A Department of State contract for the provision of IT services and goods, including hardware, software, support services, among others, for the automatic acquisition, interchange, manipulation, and transfer of data, including biometric data, worth US$350 million was designated to Alutiiq ES between 2020 and 2025. Such a broad solicitation included a contract covering equipment for “83 INM migration control checkpoints located throughout eight states in Mexico.” It is unclear whether this specific equipment included biometric identification technology.

Until 2020, it appeared Digitus was still in a “proof of concept” phase, in which the system was owned by DHS and loaned to INM. In February 2023, Digitus appears to have been handed over to the Secretary of Security and Citizen Protection (SSPC) in Mexico. There is no public information on how biometric data collected by INM is being treated, whether such data is being shared with the U.S., and or how this happens. The collection and exchange of biometric data appear to occur at specific moments, rather than continuously. For example, in 2019, in the context of the migrant caravans, the Government of Mexico shared the biometric information of nearly 12,000 people who received humanitarian visas to the US for cross-referencing with U.S. databases.

According to the response to a freedom of information request directed to the INM, the Biometric Data Sharing Program agreement signed in 2017 was still in force as of December 10, 2024. Mexican officials have refused to release statistical information related to the biometric queries received from the U.S. government and made by the Mexican Government to access stored information in U.S. databases, claiming the information is classified due to national security reasons.

Until February 2025, the INM stated biometric data was being collected at 11 migrant detention facilities located in Tapachula, Chiapas; Iztapalapa, México City; Chetumal, Quintana Roo; Guadalupe, Nuevo León; San Luis Potosí, San Luis Potosí; Villahermosa, Tabasco; Reynosa, Tamaulipas; Tampico, Tamaulipas; Hermosillo, Sonora; Acayucan, Veracruz and Zacatecas, Zacatecas. According to the INM, between 2018 and 2024, 433,224 biometric holdings have been transferred, yet there is no certainty regarding to what jurisdiction or authority such transfers have occurred 11.

US biometrics push in El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras

U.S. assistance for the adoption of biometric identification infrastructures in El Salvador, Guatemala, and Honduras has taken place over three main periods.

The initial stage involves the development of the Automated Fingerprint Identification System (AFIS) for policing agencies, as part of the Central American Fingerprint Exploitation Initiative, which is known as CAFE. Under this program, from as early as 2006 the FBI began assisting countries in developing fingerprint and biometric capabilities so as to incorporate them into the FBI’s Criminal Justice Information Services (CJIS) database. The expansion of these systems continued under the Central America Regional Security Initiative (CARSI), a counternarcotics program similar to the Mérida Initiative was established by Washington in seven countries in 2009.

The second phase of adoption of these technologies took place in the context of the creation of biometrics data-sharing partnerships, and the development of multimodal biometric identification systems, between Washington and national migration authorities from all three countries around 2019. In this period, the U.S. coordinated and promoted collaboration and data sharing on border security and migration between policing and intelligence agencies in Central America and Mexico.

Mexico, El Salvador, Honduras, and Guatemala formed the Joint Border Intelligence Group (GCIF) in collaboration with the U.S. in 2018. The GCIF allows “[Customs and Border Patrol] CBP and U.S. law enforcement access to participating countries’ criminal databases and histories of suspected gang members and criminals entering into or already in the United States.” This degree of direct access by CBP and U.S. policing authorities to government databases from the aforementioned countries was a significant expansion of previous agreements.

In 2019 security ministers of El Salvador, Honduras, Guatemala, and the head of the DHS announced their intention to expand and consolidate the use of existing communications platforms, including but not limited to the ones used to manage biometric information, aligning technology platforms to achieve interoperability and enhancing the use of GCIF as a regional model for information exchange. The signature of Biometric Data Sharing Programs in 2019 triggered US investment in the development of multimodal biometric systems to be used by migration agencies in the three nations.

The third period surveillance infrastructure development took place from 2021 to 2025 under so-called collaborative migration management policies. This phase took place in the context of the Los Angeles Declaration, which in discourse pursued promoting “safe, orderly, and humane migration” by allegedly addressing the root causes of irregular migration, expanding the legal pathways to protection and restoring asylum in the United States’ southern border. During this period, investments in biometric identification infrastructure in the region—particularly in the context of migration—were directed toward enhancing interoperability among Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador, while simultaneously expanding the capacity to collect data on individuals encountered by migration authorities at border entry points and along migration routes. These developments have contributed to the criminalization of migrant caravans and have reinforced a trend toward the quantification and surveillance of migration flows.

While similar, US backed biometric programs and corporate involvement has differed among the three Central American nations examined as part of this research. In order to tease out patterns and particularities, it is worth looking in more detail at each case.

Guatemala

In 2010 DoS-INL provided the Guatemalan National Civil Police (PNC) with an AFIS System under a contract with the US company Cogent Systems Inc specialized in providing finger, palm, face and iris biometric systems for governments. This system would be capable of centrally storing over one million fingerprints. In 2013, DoS-INL purchased additional equipment for the expansion of the AFIS System to “22 other locations” inside the Guatemalan territory. From 2010-2013, the system was in use by various Guatemalan agencies including the Ministry of the Government, under which the General Directorate of Migration operated until 2020. In 2015 a further $141,000 was invested to acquire additional hardware for the system 12.

The signing of BDSPs between Guatemala and the US led to an immediate expansion of biometric infrastructure for migration management. In 2019 Guatemala’s General Migration Office (DGM) purchased licensing for a large-scale multimodal biometric system. According to the contract requirements, the intended system would be able to facilitate fingerprint, facial, iris, palm, and voice with one to one (1:1) and one to many (1:n) matching capabilities. The system, provided through the intermediary company General Security SA was MegaMatcher, developed by the Lithuanian company Neurotechnology, specialized, according to its website, in the use of neural networks for applications such as biometric person identification and artificial intelligence. Additional purchases related to biometric systems by the DGM that year included the centralization of the migration information system into a Migration Control System (SIOM) and the expansion of border biometric data collection through the purchase of 300-high definition cameras, as well as the purchase of 11 Kojak fingerprint scanners to be used for “passport and migration control”.

In 2020, DoS-INL provided biometric workstations under a contract with Alutiiq Information Management for $187,000 to provide equipment to facilitate the collection of biometric and biographical data from individuals encountered by the Guatemalan immigration officers.

In addition, under the BDSP Guatemala received over $500,000 in software and equipment from the US between May 2021 and February 2024. Donations included Kojak fingerprint scanners for border entry points, statistical analysis software, and computing and biometric equipment.

In 2022 DoS-INL published a statement of work for the update of Guatemala’s AFIS system. According to the statement, the update would include the provision of “interconnection between AFIS systems in the Northern Triangle countries” to get “information about the people with criminal records who are in the AFIS criminal database of each participant country.” This system was provided under a contract for $472,000 awarded to the company Grupo Vision Inversiones Guatemaltecas SA, part of Grupo Vision, a company based in Honduras with regional operations. According to its website, the company has expertise in “biometric identification” and “safe cities” projects.

In December of that same year, the AFIS Secura system was launched by the PNC, the Ministry of Government, DoS-INL officials, and the U.S. Embassy. The system, which was funded by DoS-INL, unified the AFIS database with neighboring countries. Proponents claimed to be able to identify individuals through fingerprints, tattoos, scars, and height. Since 2022, Grupo Vision has supplied an additional 113 biometric devices to different units of the PNC.

El Salvador

In 2009, an AFIS system was donated to El Salvador by the U.S. for use in policing. Initially, the system included a central system and 10 terminals aimed to achieve the “automation of all ten fingerprints of people who have been detained in the country.”

The biometric system was updated to increase storage capacity and points of access in 2011-2012 under a contract awarded to 3M Cogent a company owned by Thales SA a French Company, dedicated to the development of information systems used in aerospace, defense and security, for $1.3 million, funded by the Government of Japan. In 2015 DoS-INL solicited an expansion of the existing AFIS system, and the following year an additional contract for $57,100 was awarded to the same company for increased biometric scanner capabilities for El Salvador’s National Civil Police (PNC).

In 2021 the PNC further expanded its biometric identification capabilities by purchasing two biometric enrollment workstations with fingerprint and facial capture capabilities, connected to the AFIS system from Grupo Vision de El Salvador SA de CV, for $759,423. The contract specified compliance with the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) and FBI standards.

In 2016, El Salvador’s General Migration Directorate (DGME by its acronym in Spanish) listed in among its assets a Megamatcher AFIS system. That same year the Ministry of Justice and Public Security (MJPS), which has administrative responsibility over the GMD, acquired an AFIS server worth $170,000 and licenses worth $20,000 from General Security SV, a company that operates regionally as a distributor for Neurotechnology.

By November 2018 the General Migration Directorate (DGME) was operating two AFIS servers with fingerprint and face recognition, accumulating 47 million fingerprints and 1.9 million facial captures since 2007 when the system was initiated. With partial financing from the United States’ government, the DGME purchased an additional AFIS server and 75 licenses for its Megamatcher system from General Security SV, costing $207,572.13. The system was intended to be used in migration management, aiming for the “improvement of monitoring of [the] regular flow of people entering and leaving via airports, land borders, and sea ports.” The contract requirements also describe the need to “capture and electronically send biometric characteristics of all people who cross the borders” in adherence to the Central American Customs Union and data-sharing collaboration with partner countries.

In 2020, Gissell Pérez Artiga, a representative of El Salvador’s Joint Border Intelligence Group (Grupo Conjunto de Inteligencia Fronteriza, GCIF), criminalized migrant caravans departing from Honduras and El Salvador by framing them as being co-opted by organized crime groups to facilitate movement toward the U.S. border. According to the representative for GCIF data being shared under the GCIF led to the identification of caravan members as allegedly “gang members”.

Under the Nayib Bukele administration’s “Territorial Control Plan” (Plan Control Territorial), which purports to be an anti-gang security model is based on militarization and the suspension of civil rights, the MJPS acquired mobile and fingerprint scanners from the supplier General Security worth $38,000. This contract required compliance with NIST and FBI standards and included a technology called “FaceID”, suggesting it has facial recognition capabilities.

In 2023 DGME awarded a $65,921.32 contract to General Security SV for the maintenance of AFIS servers “Megamatcher 6 and 7.” In 2024, it purchased 43 additional fingerprint scanners for $50,560.99.

Honduras

Shortly after the establishment of the National Migration Institute (INM) in Honduras in 2014, a plan to develop a Comprehensive Biometric Migration Control System to host AFIS and facial recognition was released. In 2015, the Institute issued an international public bid for acquiring a migration control system as well as a system for the issuance of passports. There is no further information publicly available on what company was awarded the contract and provided the system, but it appears to be 3M Cogent Technology, according to responses by the INM on the details of the system specifications during the international public bid process.

In 2014 local media reported that Grupo Vision supplied Honduras’ INM with biometric scanners for four airports in the country. According to the report, by September, 2014, Honduras had collected biometrics for over 45 thousand people. In 2017, Honduras INM bought approximately USD$940,000 in equipment, licenses, technical support, communications, installation and maintenance services for its biometric migration control system from Grupo Vision Honduras.

In 2016 and in 2021, it appears INM Honduras purchased biometric equipment for its regional offices. In 2022 the Honduran Migration Institute issued a public bid for the procurement of equipment, including fingerprint scanners for 32 migration control stations. The procurement appears to have been discontinued.

In 2018, INM Honduras launched a program for the “Modernization, Improvement, Management and Operation of the Migration Control System and Emission of Honduran Passports.” By 2023, the Institute had 13 different information systems, including a Biometric Migration Control System, all of which were part of its Comprehensive Migration System (Sistema Integral Migratorio, ‘SIM’).

The INM has two data centers. The first one is referred to as “DNII” whose operation and location is not public, but given its name, it might be related to the National Directorate of Information and Intelligence (DNII for its acronym in Spanish), the intelligence agency of the Honduran government. The second server, called “CCG,” is located at the Government Civic Center, a six-building complex located in the city of Tegucigalpa. The Civic Center congregates 35 governmental dependencies, including the INM, the Army and the Presidency Secretariat.

The aforementioned servers are connected with the Honduran National Migration Institute (INM) offices, its Center for Assistance to Irregular Migrants and its Centers for Assistance to Returned Migrants. In 2022 the Institute enrolled 188,269 irregular migrants in its Biometric Migration Control System. It was estimated this number would increase to 200,000 by 2024.

In 2023 INM Honduras issued an international public bid to increase its biometric identification capabilities by purchasing “hardware, software and supplies to implement an immigration control system with fingerprint and facial recognition.” Technical requirements published in the bid describe a cloud-based IT system to be installed in the Institute’s data center that was capable of supporting 750,000 daily transactions. The contract includes 20 fixed ‘control stations’ and four mobile stations. The contract—worth nearly $10 million—was awarded to the company Representaciones e Importaciones de Honduras S.A., a local subsidiary of Germany’s largest biometrics company Dermalog Identification Systems GMBH, dedicated to the development of hardware and software used for biometric identification.

Conclusion

It is important to contextualize this research within its initial methodological framework, which focused on analyzing mixed documentation related to Alutiiq LLC, Leidos Holdings Inc, General Dynamics Corp, Thales SA (as current owner of Cogent-branded biometric technology) and NEC Corp in Mexico, Guatemala, El Salvador and Honduras. As a result, this research cannot claim to provide a comprehensive account of all biometric-based systems currently deployed for migration management in the region and does not capture the role other companies have had in developing these systems as part of U.S. border externalization.

Nonetheless, it offers valuable documentary evidence concerning the expansion of biometric infrastructures facilitated by U.S. assistance in Mexico and Central America. Specifically, the research traces the historical development of these systems, identifies key providers and manufacturers, and analyzes the infrastructural architectures and capabilities involved. Together, this information helps foster a deeper understanding of the technological and political dimensions of migration control in the region.

The trajectory of biometric system adoption in the region suggests Mexico’s implementation and expansion of biometric technologies for migration control—facilitated through U.S. assistance—has established a precedent for neighboring countries.

This influence is reflected in recent U.S. agreements with Costa Rica, Panama, Colombia, and El Salvador that enable the transfer of migrants’ biometric data. Recent negotiations reportedly include provisions for expanding U.S. access to the biometric data of the general population of Mexico, sounding serious alarm bells about sovereignty, data governance, and the extraterritorial reach of U.S. security and migration policies.

There remains an urgent need to uncover and critically examine the role biometric systems and transnational data-sharing networks play in contemporary migration surveillance, as well as in shaping access to refugee protections.

Communication on the part of the U.S. CBP during the arrival of migrant caravans to the U.S.–Mexico border in 2019 provides important insights into the operational coordination between Mexican and U.S. authorities. Biometric data was shared and used to surveil and criminalize migration caravans, highlighting potential impacts on individuals’ rights to movement and access to asylum.

Recent developments in the use of databases, surveillance tools, and identification systems such as HART by ICE to locate and deport individuals from within the United States underscore pressing concerns about the regional collection and transfer of personal data.

The transnational data flows engineered in the U.S. and applied in Mexico and Central America risk reinforcing and enabling such enforcement practices. In the context of rising authoritarianism and collusion between state actors and organized crime across parts of the region, the mass deployment of biometric identification systems carries with it profound risks.

These systems may be repurposed by authoritarian regimes to target political opponents, including those in exile, amplifying threats to human rights and the safety of vulnerable populations.
